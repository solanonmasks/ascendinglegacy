Most travel medical policies include a sentence like this: *pre-existing conditions are covered if they have been stable for 90 days before the policy starts.* That one sentence decides whether a lot of claims get paid. Here is what it means.

## What is a pre-existing condition?

Any medical condition that existed before your policy started. High blood pressure, diabetes, a heart condition and asthma are common examples, but it can be anything you have seen a doctor about.

## What does "stable" mean?

Each policy defines it, and the details matter. Usually a condition is stable if, during the stability period before the policy starts, there has been:

- no new symptoms, and no worsening of existing ones
- no new diagnosis
- no new treatment, and no change in treatment
- no change in medication or dosage
- no new test results showing the condition has changed, and no tests or referrals waiting for results
- no hospital stay for the condition

The stability period is often between 90 and 365 days, depending on the policy and the traveller's age.

## Where people get caught out

**A change in medication.** Even a dose being lowered can count as a change. Some policies make an exception for routine adjustments of certain medicines; many do not.

**A test or specialist referral that was pending.** Waiting for results can mean the condition is not considered stable.

**Answering the medical questions from memory.** If your answers are wrong, even by accident, the insurer may refuse a claim.

## How to protect yourself

1. Have your medication list and recent medical history in front of you when you apply.
2. Ask your doctor or pharmacist about any changes in the last year.
3. Read the stability clause, not just the summary.
4. Tell your advisor about any change between buying the policy and leaving.
